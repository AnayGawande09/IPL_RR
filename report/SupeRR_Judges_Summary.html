Who Rajasthan Royals should back

We first measured RR's own pressure record in IPL 2025–26 against the rest of the league, to see where RR are short:

  • Biggest gap: finishing chases. In overs 16–20 of pressure chases RR strike at 168.2 against 175.4 for other teams, hit fewer boundaries (21.2% vs 24.7% of balls) and lose far more win probability.
  • Second gap: pace bowling. RR's pace bowlers take a wicket every 24.0 pressure balls (league 21.1), and RR bowl fewer dot balls at the death (25.3% vs 28.7%).
  • Strength: spin. RR's spinners take a pressure wicket every 13.8 balls (league 19.5), so more spin adds depth, not a fix.

How we found them

1. What "pressure" means

Pressure is not "the last five overs". We built a win-probability model from the data (the same idea broadcasters use for win predictors), then asked of every ball: how much could this one ball change who wins? That number is the Leverage Index, borrowed from baseball analytics. An average ball scores 1; we call a ball high pressure when it scores 1.5 or more. Games already decided are removed so nobody gains from slogging in a lost cause.

It behaves the way pressure should: on these balls batters are dismissed 20% more often (6.201% vs 5.159% per ball), and pressure builds through a run chase.

2. How performance is measured fairly

  • Every ball is worth what it did to the team's chance of winning, and to its expected runs.
  • Results are adjusted for who you faced (a spell against a top IPL batting line-up counts for more than one against a weak team) and for the stage of the innings.
  • Small samples are pulled toward the player's normal level (a long-established statistical technique, James–Stein shrinkage), so a lucky few overs cannot top the list.
  • Four measures are combined: impact on winning, run value, how much better than usual the player is under pressure, and how often they deliver.
  • To qualify, a player needs at least 60 pressure balls (10 overs), at least 6 innings with pressure balls, and a match in 2025 or 2026.

3. How the final order was set

The rating was computed three ways (all data, IPL only, recent seasons weighted more) and averaged, then stress-tested by re-running it 300 times on re-sampled matches and 4,000 times with random weightings. A player's "stays top 10" figure comes from these re-runs.

Does it predict the future?

We rebuilt every rating using only 2022–24 and compared it with what players actually did under pressure in 2025–26. For batters every predictor was close to zero (our rating +0.07, best: raw pressure strike rate +0.09), so pressure performance a season or two ahead is barely predictable at these sample sizes. For bowlers, overall run value (ignores pressure) predicted best (ρ +0.50, interval above zero) against our rating's +0.20. By component, the shrunk pressure run value (C2) carried forward best within our rating (+0.07 batters, +0.30 bowlers), while the clutch delta did not (+0.00, -0.03).

What this means. Pressure performance is only partly a stable skill; a player's underlying quality is the most reliable signal. That is why our method pulls every pressure number toward the player's own normal level, and why we flag players whose rank depends on the less repeatable "clutch" part of the score. We did not tune the lists to this one test, because that would be fitting to the answer.

A separate check: a machine-learning model on public data

As an independent comparison we trained a separate model on every IPL match 2008–2026 (open data from Cricsheet, 1,243 matches) plus public IPL auction prices 2013–2025, with its own simple definition of pressure (death overs, or chases needing 9+ an over). Where the two agree. The public model's own 2027 lists share 3 batters and 2 bowlers with ours (Shreyas Iyer, Suryakumar Yadav and Abhishek Sharma; Sunil Narine and Bhuvneshwar Kumar). Its batting list leans toward expensive top-order batters, and its bowling model found almost no repeatable signal, so we keep our lists, which also use T20I evidence and are matched to RR's needs.

What the data cannot tell us

  • Small samples. Even the busiest players face only a few hundred pressure balls. Under 100: Abhishek Sharma (98), Rajat Patidar (81) and Sufiyan Muqeem (84).
  • No IPL record. Sufiyan Muqeem and Mohammad Nawaz are rated on T20I evidence only.
  • First-innings pressure. By this measure no single first-innings ball reaches high pressure; big swings there build up over many balls.
  • Price and availability. The data has no auction prices, injuries or 2027 retention decisions. These must be checked before bidding.
  • Data correction. An updated ball file (November 2026) added the real extras runs; every result here uses it.

Glossary

TermPlain meaning
Win probabilityThe batting side's chance of winning before a ball, from a model fitted to these matches
Leverage Index (LI)How much one ball can change the win probability, relative to an average ball (1 = average)
Pressure ballA ball with LI of 1.5 or more, in a game not already decided
Run valueRuns scored minus the batting resources used up (a wicket uses a lot)
ShrinkagePulling a small-sample number toward a sensible centre, so luck counts for less
Stays top 10Share of 300 re-sampled re-runs in which the player is still top 10
